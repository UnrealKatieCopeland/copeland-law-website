Why This Matters Now

The Opportunity

I have a live, fact-developed record showing repeated refusal to provide a coherent Title II-compliant accommodation pathway for court users in ongoing Tarrant County proceedings. Hearings are pending. The issue is not a single adverse ruling — it is structural: the system processes hearings before it processes access.

An open records response confirmed that Tarrant County cannot produce its ADA Title II Self-Evaluation — a document due by January 26, 1993. The county's own Assistant Administrator acknowledged in writing that he does not know how courts handle accommodation requests. This is not a gap in one judge's practice. It is a gap in the county's compliance architecture.

"I am not aware of how courts handle ADA accommodation requests."
— Tarrant County Assistant Administrator, written response, April 1, 2026

Legal Framework

The Legal Frame

  • Title II applies to access to courts. Tennessee v. Lane, 541 U.S. 509 (2004) held that Congress validly abrogated state sovereign immunity for claims of denial of access to judicial proceedings. The obligation is not discretionary.
  • Public entities must maintain administrative compliance infrastructure. 28 C.F.R. §§ 35.105–.107 require a completed self-evaluation, public notice of rights, a designated ADA Coordinator, and a grievance procedure. These are non-delegable duties of the entity — not of individual judges.
  • The recurring failure is structural, not adjudicative. There is no reliable intake process, no accountable coordinator for court users, no written determinations before proceedings move forward, and no grievance path. The absence of process is itself the violation.
  • "Judge discretion" does not satisfy the entity's infrastructure duty. A judge's in-proceeding rulings do not substitute for the county's obligation to build and maintain the access pathway that precedes and supports those rulings.

What Relief Looks Like

The Narrow Relief Concept

The goal is prospective, process-focused relief — not a reversal of any judge's merits ruling. Specifically:

  • A designated, identifiable ADA Coordinator for court users (not just county employees)
  • A written intake and routing procedure for accommodation requests before hearings proceed
  • A confidentiality procedure for disability-related information
  • A grievance path with a named decision-maker and response timeline
  • Written determinations on accommodation requests before proceedings move forward

This relief does not ask any court to overrule the merits of any judge's adjudication. It asks the entity to build what federal law required it to build in 1993.

Time-Sensitive
Imminent Harm

Why Now

Hearings and deadlines continue while access issues remain unresolved. Each proceeding that moves forward without a functioning accommodation pathway creates avoidable procedural harm and a distorted record — not because any judge acted in bad faith, but because the infrastructure that should precede their decisions does not exist.

The harm is prospective and recurring, not retrospective. Every future hearing in Tarrant County courts carries the same structural risk for every person with a disability who appears there.

Systemic Impact

Why It Matters Beyond This Case

If framed cleanly, this case could establish at the Fifth Circuit level that "judge discretion" does not erase the public entity's non-delegable Title II infrastructure duties — especially where the absence of process predictably distorts proceedings for people with disabilities.

The question is not whether any particular judge was wrong. The question is whether a county can satisfy Title II by pointing to its judges' individual rulings when it has never built the coordinator, intake, or grievance infrastructure the regulation requires. The answer, under the text of the regulation and the holding of Tennessee v. Lane, is no.

This is a replicable, teachable framework. The Copeland Questions™ — a structured eight-question audit of what a court knew, asked, did, and documented — can be applied to any court system in any jurisdiction. A favorable ruling here creates a template for systemic reform.
